# 3. Content and Tone

The underlying cause is regression to the mean: a model produces the most generic statement that fits, so text drifts from specific facts toward broad claims.

## 3.1 Inflated significance

Telling the reader that something is important, transformative or a turning point instead of showing what it changes. Hedged versions concede that something is small and then claim it matters anyway.

## 3.2 Vague attribution

"Experts argue", "studies show", "observers have noted", "several sources" when one or no source is cited. In papers: a claim about "prior work" with no citation, or a citation that does not support the exact claim.

## 3.3 Vague connection words

"in connection with", "associated with", "linked to" where the actual relation (causes, measures, contains) is known.

## 3.4 Generic claims without numbers

Statements such as "significantly improves performance" or "achieves strong results" where a concrete figure is available. Human technical writing names the number, the dataset and the comparison.

## 3.5 Hedging disclaimers

- "While specific details are limited ...", "based on available information ..."
- "It is important to note that ..."
- Corrective framings: "X should be treated as Y rather than Z"; "this does not by itself establish ...".

## 3.6 Formulaic sections

- "Despite its strengths, X faces several challenges ..." followed by an equally generic "Future outlook".
- A closing paragraph beginning "In summary", "In conclusion" or "Overall" that repeats the body.
- Every paragraph ending in a one-line moral.

## 3.7 Over-smooth cohesion

Kumar et al. hypothesise that ChatGPT abstracts are more cohesive and less varied than human ones, while human authors cut repetition. They also found human abstracts have slightly more sentences and words. Their test sets were small, so treat this as a weak signal.

## 3.8 Talking about the text instead of the work

"This section explores ...", "We now turn to ...", "As discussed above ..." used heavily. One roadmap paragraph in the introduction is standard in IEEE papers and is not a sign.
