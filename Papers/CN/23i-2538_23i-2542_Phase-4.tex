\title{Trust, but Verify the Label: Encrypted-Traffic Misclassification as the Residual Risk of Deterministic Policy Gating}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Muhammad Abdullah Aamir$^{\ast}$ and Haider Farooq Toor}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Data Science, FAST-NUCES, Islamabad, Pakistan\\
$^{\ast}$\contactemail}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Language-model agents are being proposed for network operations, where an agent turns an operator request and a traffic label into an action such as allowing, blocking or rate-limiting a flow. When the label comes from an encrypted-traffic classifier, a policy check placed before execution can only be as correct as the prediction it reads. We measure this dependency on ISCXVPN2016. A Random Forest on 23 time-related flow statistics reaches 0.837 macro-F1 over seven applications and 0.855 weighted precision under the dataset's original protocol, against 0.808 for a rebuilt C4.5 baseline. These predictions drive a 3B-parameter local agent whose proposals pass a deterministic gate enforcing three application rules. On 100 identical proposals, the gate lowers executed violations from 30 to 6 (95\% CI of the difference 17.7--30.4 points) and keeps 97.9\% of completed tasks. All six remaining violations are restricted flows predicted as permissive classes; with true labels none remain. A probability-aware gate removes five of them only when 9.5\% of flows may be over-restricted.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Encrypted Traffic Classification, Network Policy Enforcement, Language-Model Agents, Random Forest, Deterministic Gating.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Most traffic on enterprise and campus networks is now encrypted, which removes payload inspection as a basis for application-aware policy. Classifiers trained on flow-level timing and volume statistics have therefore become the standard substitute, ranging from decision trees on time-related features \cite{drapergil2016} to deep models on packet sequences \cite{rezaei2019,lotfollahi2020,elshewey2025}. At the same time, large language models (LLMs) are being placed inside network operations: as translators from operator intent to configuration \cite{wang2024netconfeval}, as multi-agent assistants for production network management \cite{wang2025confucius}, and as analysts that query traffic predictors \cite{adanza2026}. Combining the two gives an agent that reads a classifier's label for a flow and decides what to do with it.

Placing an agent between an operator and the data plane raises a control problem that existing work treats only in part. First, written policy supplied in a prompt is followed on a best-effort basis; prior studies of tool-using agents report frequent policy breaches when an instruction conflicts with the rules \cite{zwerdling2025,sah2026verifier}. Second, the remedy proposed in those studies, a check executed before each tool call \cite{zwerdling2025,wang2026agentspec}, evaluates the action against facts it is given and assumes those facts are correct. Third, in an encrypted network the decisive fact, the application a flow belongs to, is itself a prediction with a measurable error rate, and classification studies report accuracy without asking which errors change an enforcement decision \cite{drapergil2016,elshewey2025}.

To investigate this interaction, we build a complete pipeline on ISCXVPN2016 \cite{drapergil2016}: a Random Forest that labels encrypted flows, a local LLM agent that proposes one action per flow, and a deterministic gate that checks each proposal against three application rules before a simulator executes it (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Each of 100 scenarios is answered once by the agent and replayed under every enforcement condition, so differences between conditions isolate the gate. Our primary contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Base-Protocol Rebuild:} We re-implement the evaluation protocol of the dataset's original study and show that the Random Forest exceeds the rebuilt C4.5 baseline by 4.7 points of weighted precision at every flow timeout.
\item \textbf{Paired Gating Evaluation:} We quantify the effect of deterministic pre-execution gating on identical proposals, reducing executed violations from 30 to 6 with a confidence interval that excludes zero.
\item \textbf{Error Propagation Analysis:} We derive and confirm empirically that, with a correct gate, every remaining violation requires a restricted flow to be predicted as a more permissive class.
\item \textbf{Probability-Aware Gating:} We evaluate two rules that read class probabilities instead of the top label and report the resulting safety--utility trade-off under over-restriction budgets fixed on validation data.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II reviews related work. Section~III formalizes the problem and describes the method. Section~IV details the experimental setup, and Section~V reports the results. Section~VI compares our results with the base papers, Section~VII presents ablations, Section~VIII discusses implications and limitations, and Section~IX concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

\textit{Encrypted traffic classification.} Draper-Gil et al.~\cite{drapergil2016} released ISCXVPN2016 together with 23 time-related flow features and reported that C4.5 and KNN separate seven applications with and without VPN tunnelling. Later work moved to deep models on raw packets \cite{lotfollahi2020} and to stacked ensembles on HTTPS flows \cite{elshewey2025}; Rezaei and Liu \cite{rezaei2019} survey this shift. These studies optimize accuracy or F1 over all classes, whereas a policy assigns very different consequences to different confusions.

\textit{LLM agents in network operations.} NetConfEval \cite{wang2024netconfeval} measures how reliably LLMs translate requirements into formal specifications and configurations, Confucius \cite{wang2025confucius} deploys multi-agent LLM workflows with validation steps in a production network, and Adanza et al.~\cite{adanza2026} couple an agent with traffic prediction for analysis. None of these systems lets an agent act on individual flows under an explicit enforcement policy.

\textit{Pre-execution policy enforcement.} ToolGuard \cite{zwerdling2025} compiles business policy into code guards that run before each tool call, AgentSpec \cite{wang2026agentspec} expresses runtime rules in a domain-specific language, and GuardAgent \cite{xiang2025guardagent} generates guard code with a second agent. The Verifier Tax \cite{sah2026verifier} shows that LLM-based verifiers intercept most unsafe actions but add model calls. All of these assume that the facts a guard reads are correct. \section{Proposed Methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
A flow $f$ is described by a feature vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{23}$ and belongs to a true application $y \in \mathcal{A}$, where $\mathcal{A}$ contains browsing, chat, email, file transfer, P2P, streaming and VoIP. A classifier returns probabilities $p(c \mid \mathbf{x})$ for $c \in \mathcal{A}$ and the prediction $\hat{y} = \arg\max_c p(c \mid \mathbf{x})$. An agent proposes one action $a$ from $\{\textsc{allow}, \textsc{block}, \textsc{limit}(k)\}$ with rate $k$ in kbps. The operator policy maps each application to its set of permitted actions $\Pi(c)$:
\begin{equation}
\Pi(c) =
\begin{cases}
\{\textsc{block}\}, & c = \text{P2P},\\
\{\textsc{block}\} \cup L(1024), & c \in \mathcal{R}_1,\\
\{\textsc{allow}, \textsc{block}\} \cup L(10^4), & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
with $L(u) = \{\textsc{limit}(k): k \in \mathbb{Z},\ 64 \le k \le u\}$,
where $\mathcal{R}_1$ contains streaming and file transfer. A gate $G$ executes $a$ only if it passes structural checks and $a \in \Pi(\hat{y})$. An executed action is a violation when it is not permitted for the true class:
\begin{equation}
V(a, y) = \mathbb{1}\left[a \text{ executed} \wedge a \notin \Pi(y)\right].
\label{eq:violation}
\end{equation}
Because the gate executes only actions in $\Pi(\hat{y})$, a violation under a correct gate requires $a \in \Pi(\hat{y}) \setminus \Pi(y)$, which is non-empty only when $\hat{y} \neq y$ and $\Pi(\hat{y}) \not\subseteq \Pi(y)$. Under (\ref{eq:policy}) this happens exactly when a restricted flow is predicted as a class of a less restrictive level. Every residual violation is therefore attributable to a specific direction of misclassification, which Section~V tests.

\subsection{Architecture and Components}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. The classifier writes $\hat{y}$ for each flow into a trusted record that the agent can read but not modify. The agent receives the flow identifier, $\hat{y}$, the operator request and the written policy, and returns one JSON tool call; no generated code is executed. The gate reads $\hat{y}$ directly from the trusted record, and a dictionary simulator stores each flow as held, allowed, blocked or rate-limited, with every flow starting held.

\textit{Traffic classifier.} A Random Forest \cite{breiman2001} with 100 trees, maximum depth 16, at least two samples per leaf and balanced class weights is compared with a depth-10 decision tree and a majority-class predictor, using scikit-learn \cite{pedregosa2011} with seed 42. The model with the higher validation macro-F1 is frozen before testing.

\textit{Agent.} Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct \cite{grattafiori2024llama3}, quantized to 4 bits (Q4\_K\_M), runs on a laptop CPU through llama.cpp \cite{llamacpp} with temperature 0, seed 42, a 2,048-token context and at most 128 output tokens.

\textit{Deterministic gate.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:gate} gives the gate. The four checks run in order, and any failure leaves the flow held.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Deterministic gate}
\label{alg:gate}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require proposal $a$, episode flow $f^{\ast}$, trusted record $T$, simulator $S$
\If{unknown tool \textbf{or} missing argument \textbf{or} flow $\notin S$}
  \State \Return \textsc{hold} \Comment{dispatch check}
\EndIf
\If{extra arguments \textbf{or} $k \notin \mathbb{Z} \cap [64, 10^4]$}
  \State \Return \textsc{hold} \Comment{schema and range}
\EndIf
\If{flow of $a \neq f^{\ast}$} \State \Return \textsc{hold} \Comment{identity}\EndIf
\If{$a \notin \Pi(T[f^{\ast}])$} \State \Return \textsc{hold} \Comment{policy, Eq.~(\ref{eq:policy})}\EndIf
\State \Return \textsc{execute}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Probability-Aware Gating}
Because the gate in Algorithm~\ref{alg:gate} trusts $\hat{y}$, we also test two rules that replace $T[f^{\ast}]$ with a more cautious label. Let $r(c)$ be the strictness level of class $c$: 2 for P2P, 1 for $\mathcal{R}_1$ and 0 otherwise. The \emph{top-2 rule} applies the stricter of the two most likely classes when $\max_c p(c \mid \mathbf{x}) < \tau$. The \emph{policy-mass rule} applies the strictest level $\ell$ whose total probability reaches a threshold $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
M_\ell(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{c:\, r(c) \ge \ell} p(c \mid \mathbf{x}),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\ell^{\ast} = \max\big\{\ell \ge r(\hat{y}) : \ell = r(\hat{y}) \,\vee\, M_\ell(\mathbf{x}) \ge \rho\big\},
\label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
and enforces the most probable class at level $\ell^{\ast}$. Because classifier probabilities need not be calibrated \cite{guo2017calibration}, thresholds are chosen on the validation split as the value that minimizes the share of flows given a more permissive policy than their true one, subject to an over-restriction budget $\beta$ on flows given a stricter policy. We report $\beta = 5\%$ and $\beta = 10\%$, both fixed before any test scenario was scored.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textit{Dataset and preprocessing.} We use Scenario~B of ISCXVPN2016 with a 15-second flow timeout: 18,758 flows, each described by 23 statistics covering duration, forward, backward and flow inter-arrival times, active and idle periods, and packet and byte rates. No payload, address or port is used. VPN and non-VPN variants are merged into seven application labels, and the VPN flag is kept for analysis only. We remove 622 rows whose identical feature vectors carry different labels and 152 exact duplicates, leaving 17,984 flows, then draw a stratified sample of 10,000 split into 6,000 training, 2,000 validation and 2,000 test flows. The value $-1$, which marks statistics that do not apply to a flow, is kept as a value. The release carries no capture or session identifiers, so flows from one capture may fall into different splits.

\textit{Scenario suite.} Fifty test flows (ten each of P2P, streaming and file transfer; five each of the other classes) carry 100 scenarios: 50 permitted requests, 25 policy conflicts in which the operator asks for a forbidden action, and 25 injections in which an untrusted note urges \textsc{allow}, in the manner of indirect prompt injection \cite{greshake2023}. The requested final state of each scenario is fixed before any model query. Ten of the 50 flows are misclassified by the frozen Random Forest.

\textit{Conditions.} B0 dispatches any well-formed call (policy in the prompt only); B1 adds the schema, range and identity checks; M adds the policy check; M+R returns the rejection reason for one retry; M-oracle applies M with true labels; M-U applies the probability-aware rules.

\textit{Metrics.} Over $N$ scenarios the violation rate is $\bar{V} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i V(a_i, y_i)$. Completion counts permitted scenarios that end in exactly the requested state, and retention is the completion of a condition divided by that of B0. Uncertainty is estimated with 1,000 paired bootstrap resamples of the 50 base flows \cite{efron1994}, keeping all scenarios of a flow together.

\textit{Base-paper protocol.} To compare with \cite{drapergil2016} on equal terms we rebuild its setting: 14 classes, the uncleaned feature files, stratified 10-fold cross-validation, an entropy-based decision tree with at least two samples per leaf (C4.5-style) and a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier on features scaled to $[0,1]$, for all four timeouts.

\textit{Implementation.} Experiments run on an Intel Core i5-1235U laptop with 16\,GB RAM, without a GPU, using Python 3.11.9 and scikit-learn 1.8.0.

\subsection{Probability-Aware Gating}
Under a 5\% over-restriction budget, neither rule removes a violation on the test scenarios: the policy-mass threshold selected on validation ($\rho = 0.45$) keeps 6 violations and 47 completed tasks. Under a 10\% budget, validation selects $\rho = 0.25$, and violations fall to 1 while completed tasks fall to 43, a retention of 89.6\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:unc}). The top-2 rule performs worse at both budgets because, for four of the six flows, the second most likely class is also permissive. The remaining violation is a P2P flow assigned 0.001 probability of being P2P, a confident error that no probability rule can recover.

\section{Ablation Study}

\textit{Enforcement layers.} Moving from B0 to B1 removes 3 of 30 violations; adding the policy check removes 21 more. Most of the gate's value therefore comes from the policy rules, while structural checks catch a different class of error: a correct intent expressed with a wrong number.

\textit{Label source.} Replacing $\hat{y}$ with $y$ (M-oracle) removes all six residual violations and the single wrongful refusal, confirming the analysis in Section~III-A.

\textit{Retry.} Returning the rejection reason recovers 4 of 25 rejected scenarios but introduces one new violation: a P2P flow predicted as file transfer is rate-limited at 512\,kbps, a rate permitted for the predicted class only.

\textit{Classifier and timeout.} Replacing the Random Forest with the decision tree lowers macro-F1 by 0.095. Across the four timeouts the Random Forest stays between 0.811 and 0.861 macro-F1 and leads the decision tree at each, so the choice of a 15-second window does not drive the results.

\textit{Features and VPN transfer.} Fig.~\ref{fig:rob}(a) removes one feature group at a time. Forward and backward inter-arrival times (0.030) and packet and byte rates (0.025) are the only groups whose removal costs more than 0.012 macro-F1. Fig.~\ref{fig:rob}(b) trains on one tunnel type and tests on the other: a model trained on non-VPN flows reaches 0.458 macro-F1 on VPN flows, against 0.806 when VPN flows are included in training.

\section{Discussion}

The gate is effective because the policy is a short lookup from application to permitted actions, which code evaluates exactly and in microseconds, while the agent subordinates the written policy to the operator's instruction in every conflict scenario. The weakness is structural rather than computational: by (\ref{eq:violation}), a correct gate cannot produce a violation unless the label it reads moves a flow to a more permissive class. For operators this changes what classifier quality should mean. Overall accuracy matters less than the recall of restricted classes against permissive ones, and the 13.6\% rate at which file transfer is read as chat contributes more risk than any confusion among permissive classes. The VPN transfer result adds a deployment condition: a classifier in front of a policy gate must be trained on tunnelled traffic, because tunnelling reshapes the timing statistics the classifier relies on.

Probability-aware gating shows that the classifier's confidence carries usable signal, since all six violating flows are low-confidence predictions, but exploiting it costs legitimate work. At the 10\% budget, five violations disappear and four permitted tasks are lost, and one confident error remains out of reach of any threshold.

\textit{Threats to validity.} The study uses one 3B-parameter model, one dataset recorded in 2016 without capture identifiers, a simulated data plane, 100 scenarios written by the authors and three static rules. Counts in Table~\ref{tab:viol} are small, so they describe the mechanism rather than estimate a population rate. The research objective, measuring how classification errors propagate through deterministic enforcement, is met within these limits.

\section{Conclusion}

A deterministic gate placed between an LLM agent and an encrypted network reduced executed policy violations from 30 to 6 on identical proposals while preserving 97.9\% of completed tasks, and the Random Forest that labels flows exceeded the dataset's original classifiers under their own protocol. Every violation that passed the gate came from a restricted flow labelled as a more permissive class, and probability-aware gating traded four legitimate tasks for five of those six. Future work will measure the same pipeline on recent traffic with capture-separated splits, add stateful rules such as bandwidth budgets, and train classifiers with costs that reflect policy levels rather than uniform accuracy.
